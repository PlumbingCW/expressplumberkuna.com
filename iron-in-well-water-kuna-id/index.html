Iron and Rust-Colored Water in Kuna: City Water, Wells and Filters

Orange or brown water has more than one cause. Here is how to tell iron in the water from rusty pipes or a tired water heater, and what filtration can honestly do.

By the Kuna Express Plumbing teamPublished October 15, 2026
Plumbing problem right now?(208) 679-8357Call Now

Rust-colored water comes from one of four places. It may be iron in the source water, rust inside old steel or galvanized pipe, a corroding water heater, or sediment stirred up by a change in the supply. The quickest sorting test is to run a cold tap and a hot tap separately and compare them in a clean white container.

If only the hot water is discolored, suspect the water heater. If both are discolored and you are on city water, call the City of Kuna Water Department before spending money. If you have a private well, a lab test comes first, and then the filter choice.

Sort the cause with a simple test

You do not need tools to narrow this down. Fill a clear glass or a white bucket from the cold tap, then the hot tap, and look at both in daylight.

What you seeLikely causeFirst step
Orange water from the hot tap onlyRust or sediment from the water heater tankHave the heater inspected
Brown or orange from the cold tap, all tapsIron in the source, or rust in the supply pipeCity water: call the Water Department. Well: test the water
Discolor only after the water sat unusedRust in older steel or galvanized pipeRun the tap for a minute, then ask about a pipe check
Water clears quickly, then returnsSediment disturbed by a change in supplyNote the date and ask your provider
Reddish stains on fixtures and laundryIron staining over timeTest the water and consider treatment

Take a photo of the glass with a timestamp. A plumber, a lab or the city can learn a lot from how the color looks and how quickly it settles.

City water or a private well

The City of Kuna Water Department runs several wells, and its December 2021 Consumer Confidence Report lists nine of them, drawing groundwater. If your home is on city service, rusty water is something to report to the department. Staff can tell you whether work on the system may have stirred up sediment.

If your home is not on city service and you have your own well, the water quality is your responsibility. That is where testing matters most, and where a filtration plan starts.

Iron in a private well

Iron is common in groundwater. It can be dissolved and clear when it comes out of the tap, then turn orange after it meets air, or it can already be particles that settle in a glass. The form of the iron decides which treatment works, which is why the test comes first.

Water with iron can stain sinks, toilets, laundry and fixtures. Whether it also affects taste or the appliances that use water depends on the amount. A lab can report the level, and we do not guess it for you.

Testing a private well near Kuna

Central District Health says private well owners should test their water once a year for bacteria and nitrates. It also says harmful levels often cannot be detected by taste, sight or smell, so a clear glass is not proof that the water is safe.

That matters for iron too. Rust color tells you about iron, not about bacteria or nitrates, so ask the lab to run the annual tests and add iron to the list. Central District Health does not provide test kits, but it points to labs such as Analytical Laboratories or the Idaho Department of Laboratories. Its general line is (208) 375-5211.

Source: Central District Health

What the filters can honestly do

No filter fixes every kind of iron. The right choice depends on the form and the amount, along with hardness, pH and household use. Here is how the common options compare.

OptionWorks best forHonest limit
Sediment filterParticles of rust that are already solidDoes not remove dissolved iron
Water softenerSmall amounts of dissolved iron along with hardnessCan foul with heavier iron and may need a different setup
Oxidizing iron filterDissolved iron that is converted to particles and filtered outNeeds sizing, backwashing and upkeep
Chemical feed and filterHigher iron or iron with other problemsMore equipment to maintain
Point-of-use filterDrinking or cooking water at one tapDoes not protect the whole house

A plumber should size the equipment from your lab report. If a salesperson quotes a system without asking for one, ask why.

Rusty pipes are a different problem

If the water is clean at the source and rusty at the tap, the pipes may be the issue. Old galvanized steel corrodes from the inside and sheds rust, and short bursts of color after the water has sat can be a sign. In that case a filter is a bandage and the long-term answer may be new pipe.

Our page on repiping explains when replacement makes sense. A plumber can look at the pipe where it enters the house and at a cut section if one is available.

When the water heater is the culprit

Orange water from the hot side only points toward the tank. Sediment and rust can build up over time, and a leaking tank is a bigger concern than color. Check the base of the heater for moisture, and call if you find any.

Do not drain or flush a water heater unless you know how to do it safely, because the water is hot and the valves can fail. If you smell gas near the heater, leave the house and call 911 and Intermountain Gas from outside.

What to do while you decide

Do not drink or cook with water that is cloudy, smells odd or has been flagged by a test. Use bottled water until you know more. Run the cold tap until the color clears before filling a kettle or a pet bowl. Avoid washing white laundry in discolored water because the stains can set.

If a lab report shows bacteria or nitrates above safe levels, follow the lab's advice. Call Central District Health for guidance on next steps.

Choosing a softener or iron filter

Hard water and iron often travel together, so a plan can cover both. Southern Idaho water is generally hard, per the City of Kuna report, and a softener addresses that scale. For the iron part, the lab results tell the plumber whether a softener alone will do or a separate filter is needed.

Questions about iron and rust in water

The color alone does not tell you. Iron is mostly a nuisance, but the same water could carry other problems you cannot see. Use bottled water until you know the cause, and test a private well.

A softener can handle small amounts of dissolved iron, but heavier iron can foul the resin. A lab report and a plumber's sizing tell you whether you need a dedicated iron filter.

That points to the water heater tank, where rust and sediment can build up. Have it inspected, and check the base for leaks.

A pitcher filter may help the taste at one tap, but it is not built to treat a whole house for iron. For staining and heavy iron, get a lab result and talk to a plumber about whole-house options.
