Who books a planned install

Most installs fall into a handful of situations, and each one changes the work.

  • A new house or an addition that has no heater yet.
  • A tank that is old, noisy or running out of hot water and is being replaced before it leaks.
  • A switch from electric to gas, gas to electric, or a tank to a tankless unit.
  • A bigger household that has outgrown a small tank.
  • A move of the heater from a cramped closet to a garage or utility room.
  • A rental or flip where the unit has to be ready before a tenant or buyer arrives.

Tell dispatch which one fits. It tells the plumber what to bring and how long to set aside.

There is no best type for every house. A plumber looks at what is already there, because the cheapest path is usually the one that reuses the existing gas line, vent or circuit.

Fuel in Kuna and what a fuel change involves

According to the City of Kuna, Idaho Power supplies electricity and Intermountain Gas supplies natural gas. That matters for planning, because moving from electric to gas means a gas line run to the heater and a vent to the outdoors, and moving from gas to electric means a dedicated circuit and an electrician. We handle the plumbing side and tell you plainly when an electrician or the gas utility has to be part of the job.

Never connect or relight a gas appliance yourself after a fuel change. If you ever smell gas, leave the building, call 911 and Intermountain Gas, and call us from outside.

Planning checklist for an install

Decide on the fuel, note the tank size you have now, and clear a path to the heater. Check the closet or garage space for clearance and the floor for a drain. If the unit sits above living space, ask about a drain pan. Having these answers ready shortens the planning call.

Permits for water heater work in Kuna

Plumbing permits in Kuna come from the Building Department at City Hall. The City's January 2025 permit application lists water heater replacement among the work it covers and has a field for the contractor license number. For a brand new install or a fuel change, the paperwork can differ, so we ask the Kuna Building Department at (208) 922-5546 before the job instead of guessing.

The city's form says work done without a permit carries double permit fees as the minimum charge. A permit also keeps the install on record for the day you sell the house. Homeowners may buy a permit only for their own primary or secondary residence, per Idaho DOPL, and our plumbers pull permits for jobs we perform.

The parts that come with a proper install

A heater is only as safe as what surrounds it. The plumber looks at the cold-water shutoff, flexible connectors, the relief valve and its discharge line, a drain pan when the heater sits above finished space, and a thermal expansion tank where the system calls for one. Whether you need an expansion tank depends on whether the house has a closed system, such as a backflow device or pressure reducer, so we check instead of assuming.

Old connectors and corroded shutoffs are replaced during the install, because they are the next likely leak. Ask dispatch about your situation if the heater sits in an attic or above a ceiling.

Timing an install before cold weather

Boise-area records put the average first freeze in mid-October, per National Weather Service data reported by KBOI. A heater in an unheated garage or crawlspace works hardest once the incoming water turns cold, and exposed supply lines are at more risk. That is a good reason to book a planned install in autumn instead of during the first hard cold, when everyone else is calling too.

What not to do before the install

Do not drain or disconnect the old heater yourself, and do not relight a gas unit that has been off. Do not store paint, gasoline or other flammables near the heater. If you smell gas, leave and call 911 and Intermountain Gas.
