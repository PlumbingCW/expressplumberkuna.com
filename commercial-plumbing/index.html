When a business plumbing problem is an emergency

Some commercial issues can be scheduled. These cannot.

  • Water is flowing from a broken line, a failed fixture or a ceiling.
  • A floor drain, restroom or kitchen line is backing up into the building.
  • Water is pooling near electrical panels, equipment or stored goods.
  • A restroom is out of service and the building has no other.
  • A utility has locked the water or sent a notice about a leak or a backflow device.

If water is near live electrical equipment, stay out of the room and shut off power only if you can reach the panel safely. Otherwise call and wait for a plumber.

Commercial buildings are not houses

Commercial plumbing differs from residential work in several ways. Pipes are larger, fixtures are shared, and the same building may have a separate irrigation line, a fire line and a backflow assembly feeding the system.

A plumber who works on a house may not know how a commercial riser, a tenant space or a floor drain connects to the rest of the building. Tell dispatch whether you own the building, lease a suite, or manage it for someone else. That decides who can approve work and who is responsible for which line.

Grease traps, interceptors and floor drains

Any business with a grease trap or interceptor has to keep it clean. When the trap is full, fats and food waste pass through and clog the line downstream, and the first sign may be a floor drain that backs up after hours.

A plumber can open the trap, assess what is inside, and clear the outlet line. If the trap is a sizable underground unit, tell dispatch and the plumber will look at what equipment the job needs. For a repeat problem, a camera shows whether the line has damage or grease coating.

When the backup reaches the building's main line, our sewer line cleaning page explains how that clearing works. A plumber can also run a camera to see where the restriction sits.

Backflow assemblies and reports

A backflow assembly keeps water in the building from flowing backward into the public supply. Water suppliers often require the assembly to be tested on a schedule, and a missed test can end with a notice or a locked valve.

The City of Kuna sends backflow reports to its Water Department, so ask the Water Department which devices it tracks and when your test is due. A plumber tests the assembly, repairs worn parts, and prepares the report for the Water Department. The City of Kuna plumbing permit application also lists backflow devices as covered work, so ask before installing or replacing one.

Storm drains and outdoor grates

Storm drains carry rain and runoff, and they are separate from the sanitary sewer that serves your restrooms and kitchen. A clogged storm grate on private property can leave standing water in a lot or loading area. We clear the on-site run, grate and catch basin when the obstruction is debris or sediment.

Public storm drains in streets are a different matter. If the blockage is out in the right of way, call City of Kuna Public Works at (208) 287-1728 and ask who maintains it.

Permits and licensing for commercial plumbing

Commercial plumbing work in Kuna goes through the City of Kuna Building Department, and the city's plumbing permit application lists commercial and industrial plumbing as covered work. Call the Building Department at (208) 922-5546 to ask what your job needs.

Idaho's Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses is clear on one point. A homeowner permit is only valid for a person's own home and outbuildings, and it is not valid for a commercial building. Commercial work therefore goes to a licensed plumbing contractor, and for multi-family buildings the city treats the permit as contractors only.

Safe steps while you wait

Shut off the water to the affected fixture or area, and use the main valve for the building if you cannot find a smaller one. Move stock and records out of the way of the water. Do not enter standing water near electrical equipment or open a backed-up drain with chemicals. Take photographs of the damage for your insurance claim, and call.

Finding a leak on commercial property

Business owners often know roughly where the water is, but not exactly. A damp lot, a notice from the water utility or a jump in the monthly bill all suggest a buried leak. A leak detection visit locates the line before anyone digs. Large or unusual systems, such as a fire line, may need other trades alongside a plumber, and we tell you plainly when a job is outside our work.
