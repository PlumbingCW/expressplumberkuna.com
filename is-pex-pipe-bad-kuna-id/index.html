Is PEX Pipe Bad? PEX vs Copper for Kuna, ID Homes

PEX is not bad, but it is not perfect either. Here are the real strengths and weak spots of PEX and copper so you can judge a repipe quote.

By the Kuna Express Plumbing teamPublished October 15, 2026
Plumbing problem right now?(208) 679-8357Call Now

PEX is not a bad pipe. It is a flexible plastic supply pipe that plumbers have installed in homes for a long time, and it works well when it is installed correctly and kept away from the things that damage it. The bad reputation comes from a few real weak points, such as sunlight, certain fittings, and poor workmanship, plus a lot of internet noise.

Copper has its own weak points. Neither material wins in every house. The honest answer is that the pipe matters less than the installation, the water, and whether the system fits your home.

Why people ask whether PEX is bad

Most of the worry traces back to three things. Some people have seen photos of failed fittings, some have read warnings about PEX near sunlight, and some distrust anything that is not metal. These concerns are not all wrong, but they are narrower than the headlines suggest.

We are not going to quote failure statistics here, because we do not have trustworthy local numbers and invented ones would mislead you. Instead, here is how the two materials differ in ways you can verify with a plumber and a product label.

Where PEX is strong

PEX bends around corners, so a run often needs fewer fittings than copper. Fewer joints means fewer places for a leak to start. It is also quieter and it handles a small amount of freezing better than rigid pipe, because it can expand a bit without splitting at once.

Installation tends to be faster, which can reduce the disruption when walls must be opened. Because it does not corrode like metal, it does not pit from the inside the way some copper does in aggressive water.

Where PEX is weak

PEX is damaged by ultraviolet light. It should not be left exposed to sun, and an outdoor or garage run needs to be covered and protected. It can also be chewed by rodents, and it does not like contact with some solvents and petroleum products.

The fittings are the part most worth discussing. PEX relies on crimp, clamp or expansion connections, and these only work when the right tool and the right size are used for the right fitting. A poorly made connection may look fine for a while before it seeps.

Where copper is strong and weak

Copper has a long track record, handles heat well, and is not affected by sunlight or most rodents. It is rigid, so exposed runs stay neat and in place. When a copper system is built well and the water is gentle on it, it can serve a house for a very long time.

Copper has its own failure modes. It can develop pinhole leaks from the inside, and joints can fail if soldering was rushed. It is also stiffer to install, which can mean more fittings and more opened wall. Materials tend to cost more than PEX, though the right choice for a job is a call for the plumber, not a rule of thumb.

What to do if you suspect a failing pipe

Shut the water off at the main shutoff and open a faucet low in the house to relieve pressure. Photograph any wet spots, green crust on copper, or damp drywall. Do not seal a spraying pipe with tape and leave it. If a ceiling is bulging with water, call right away and keep clear of the area.

If the leak is small and slow, shut the water off anyway. A slow leak inside a wall can soak insulation and framing unseen.

Questions to ask before choosing a pipe

A good quote should tell you what is being installed and why. These questions help you compare bids without needing to be a pipefitter.

  1. Which brand and type of PEX or copper is being used, and is it rated for hot water?
  2. How will the pipe be protected from sunlight, nails and sharp edges?
  3. What fitting system is being used, and who makes the tool?
  4. Will the work be permitted and inspected, and who pulls the permit?
  5. How will walls, ceilings and the water heater connections be handled?

A plumber who answers these plainly is a better sign than one who only praises a material. Ask for the answers in writing.

Kuna water and what it means for your pipes

The City of Kuna's own water quality report says that all of southern Idaho has hard water because of naturally occurring calcium. Hard water is mostly an issue for water heaters, fixtures and aerators, and it does not disappear because you chose PEX or copper. If scale is part of your complaint, a softener question belongs next to the pipe question.

The same report lists lead and copper sampling on 9/16/2020, with 30 samples and no sites above action levels. That says something about the city's sampling, not about the plumbing in your house. Your own pipes, solder and fixtures are still worth checking if you have an older home.

When a repipe makes sense

Repiping is a big job, so it should be based on evidence and not on fear. Repeated leaks in different spots, discolored water, low flow that comes back after fixtures are cleaned, and pipe that is flaking or crusted are reasons to start the conversation. A single leak in one section often needs only a repair.

If you are considering the whole house, read about repiping in Kuna and ask for the plan in writing. Plumbing for an existing residence is covered work on the City of Kuna plumbing permit application, so ask the Kuna Building Department at (208) 922-5546 whether your project needs one.

Hard water also affects the rest of the system. If scale is shortening the life of fixtures, a water softener installation may belong in the same conversation as the pipe.
